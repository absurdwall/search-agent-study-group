# Develop Book Chapter

Build chapters by teaching carefully from excellent existing material. Put most
effort into selecting the right material, representing its content accurately,
and shaping a clear learning path. Plan the prose and visual layer together, but
do not invent a new conceptual framework or recreate a useful source visual when
the selected material already teaches the topic well.

Read:

- [references/source-led-chapter-method.md](references/source-led-chapter-method.md)
  before selecting resources, outlining, drafting, or revising prose.
- [references/visual-language.md](references/visual-language.md) before adding
  visual placeholders, tables, figures, callouts, animation, or interaction.

## Preserve user ownership

Treat the user as the owner of:

- audience, learning outcome, curriculum, and chapter scope;
- which resources are core, extended, or assigned;
- visual direction and acceptable reuse decisions;
- chapter status, readiness, merge, and publication.

Propose choices with reasons. Do not silently make these decisions.

Start discussion-first. Do not edit the chapter until the user approves a
concrete change. Do not search for, generate, download, or embed media until the
corresponding visual job and placement are approved.

## Workflow

### 1. Orient to the current book

Read the project instructions, current chapter, neighboring chapter boundaries,
book landing page, shared styles, visual/source notes, and relevant weekly page.
Inspect the full named artifact before proposing changes.

Confirm the current worktree and preserve unrelated or concurrent work. Keep
raw captures and user-authored notes unchanged unless explicitly asked.

### 2. Establish the chapter contract

Agree with the user on:

- intended reader and prior knowledge;
- learning outcome;
- scope and exclusions;
- what the reader should be able to explain or reconstruct afterward;
- boundaries with adjacent chapters.

Do not use research to expand the approved scope.

### 3. Choose the teaching-resource spine

Research or inspect candidate teaching materials before drafting. Prefer a
small number of strong resources that already explain the topic clearly.

Recommend the best candidate or small combination and explain:

- what each resource teaches well;
- which chapter sections it can anchor;
- what must be adapted for this book;
- what gaps require a supporting source or original connective explanation.

Obtain user approval for the resource spine. Do not treat a recommended
resource as assigned reading without approval.

Create a section-level source map using the template in the source-led method
reference. Follow the selected material faithfully while adapting sequence,
terminology, and depth to the approved chapter contract. Avoid a patchwork of
loosely related facts from many sources. Record useful figures, animations,
videos, or interactions already present in the selected resources.

### 4. Approve the narrative before polishing prose

Propose the narrative spine and section sequence. For each section, identify:

- its teaching question;
- its primary source and exact relevant portion;
- the mechanism, example, or distinction to preserve;
- necessary connective explanation;
- scope boundaries and common misconceptions;
- likely visual opportunities.

Obtain approval before substantial prose work.

### 5. Draft prose and visual placeholders together

After the section's teaching content and source coverage are clear, add detailed
visual placeholders directly at their intended positions in the chapter page.
Do not leave visual planning only in an internal notes file.

Prefer borrowing, embedding, linking, or adapting an approved visual from the
selected teaching resource when it already performs the intended job. Visuals
can make the chapter inviting and memorable, but original visual production is
not the center of the workflow.

Each placeholder must describe the image, animation, video, or interaction
carefully enough to guide source selection and implementation. When original
production is necessary, it must also be specific enough to serve as:

- an image-generation prompt;
- an animation storyboard;
- a media-search brief; or
- a designer/developer handoff.

Include the required fields from the visual-language reference. Keep a matching
entry in the chapter's visual notes when production details, provenance, or
later decisions need to persist.

Review the draft page with the user before producing media. Confirm placement,
visual density, teaching jobs, and duplication.

### 6. Develop one section or visual at a time

Work incrementally:

1. Confirm the approved section or visual.
2. Make only the bounded change.
3. Refresh the stable local preview.
4. Check the result with the user.
5. Record unresolved decisions without reopening settled work.

For an approved visual, evaluate the selected teaching resource and other
accurate reusable source material first. Reuse or link the strongest suitable
visual with correct attribution and permission handling. Generate or build an
original visual only when existing visuals cannot perform the teaching job, the
new visual is necessary, and its required content is already clear. Replace the
placeholder only after the result is reviewed.

### 7. Place references according to their job

Keep source roles distinct:

- Put claim-level citations beside the supported claim and link directly to the
  supporting page or section.
- Identify primary companion reading near the chapter opening when one or two
  resources form the teaching spine.
- List every cited source in the reference section.
- Put related but uncited material only in Extended references.
- Attribute every external figure in its caption and provenance record.

Label product-specific examples and book-specific synthesis honestly. Do not
use a product source to prove a universal taxonomy.

### 8. Validate the reading experience

Validate in proportion to the change:

- chapter outcome, narrative continuity, and reconstruction goal;
- chapter boundaries and terminology;
- claim-to-source coverage and reference roles;
- visual placement, captions, attribution, and text alternatives;
- semantic tables and preserved row/column relationships;
- desktop and approximately 390px mobile layouts;
- keyboard focus, ARIA, reduced motion, and no-JavaScript fallback;
- local links, assets, console errors, and page-level overflow.

Use the actual rendered page for visual and interaction claims.

### 9. Converge without deciding readiness

Separate findings into:

- required corrections;
- user-approved finishing work;
- optional ideas to park.

Do not declare the chapter ready, published, assigned, frozen, or complete on
the user's behalf. Do not commit, push, merge, or publish without explicit
authorization.

## Approval checkpoints

Pause for user approval before:

- changing the chapter contract or neighboring chapter boundaries;
- adopting the teaching-resource spine;
- changing the narrative structure;
- making the first concrete chapter edit;
- researching, generating, downloading, or embedding a visual;
- replacing an approved visual placeholder;
- changing shared styles or another chapter;
- changing a readiness label, assignment, branch, merge, or publication state.

## Avoid these failure modes

- Writing an independent explanation first and adding sources afterward.
- Accumulating references merely because they are relevant.
- Copying a source's product taxonomy as a universal model.
- Spending visual-production effort before the teaching content is correct.
- Recreating a useful visual that the selected resource already provides.
- Deferring all visual thinking until after prose is finished.
- Using vague placeholders such as “add diagram here.”
- Hiding visual plans only in internal notes so the draft rhythm cannot be
  reviewed.
- Treating visual excitement as decoration rather than part of learning.
- Converting true sequences into tables or true tables into mobile cards.
- Letting optional polish repeatedly reopen approved work.
