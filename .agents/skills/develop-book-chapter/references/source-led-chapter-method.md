# Source-led chapter method

## Core principle

Teach from excellent existing material. Select the right resource, understand
how it teaches the concept, cover and adapt it carefully, and place references
where readers can verify and continue learning.

Do not create a new conceptual framework merely to sound original. Add original
text primarily to:

- connect selected resources;
- fit the chapter's audience and approved scope;
- clarify a responsibility boundary or misconception;
- provide a consistent example;
- explain why a product-specific account is not universal.

## Select the teaching spine

Evaluate a candidate resource for:

- conceptual accuracy;
- teaching clarity and sequence;
- suitability for the chapter's audience;
- useful examples, figures, or interactive explanations;
- stable and direct citation targets;
- product specificity and stated limitations;
- visual reuse or attribution constraints.

Prefer one strong resource, or a small complementary set, over a source soup.
An authoritative specification may verify a claim without being the best
teaching spine. A practitioner resource may motivate a chapter without defining
its terms.

Distinguish these roles:

- **Teaching spine:** the material whose explanatory path substantially shapes
  the chapter.
- **Verification source:** a primary source used to support a specific claim.
- **Origin source:** the first proposal or launch material, useful for explaining
  the problem a mechanism originally set out to solve.
- **Current-state source:** the latest official specification or documentation,
  used to prevent the origin story from being mistaken for present behavior.
- **Product example:** documentation showing one concrete implementation.
- **Practitioner perspective:** useful experience, explicitly non-canonical.
- **Extended reading:** relevant material not cited in the chapter.

Treat useful visuals as part of resource selection. A resource that already
contains an accurate, well-taught diagram, animation, video, or interaction may
reduce the need for original visual production.

## Build the source map

Before substantial prose, prepare a compact map:

| Section | Teaching job | Primary resource and exact portion | What to preserve | What to adapt or add | Claim citations | Visual opportunity | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example | Explain one tool round trip | Direct source and relevant section | Responsibility sequence | Book terminology and transition | Supporting direct links | Five-state sequence | Do not teach the repeated loop yet |

Use exact page or section links where possible. Inspect neighboring chapter
source ownership before adding a resource.

For each visual opportunity, record whether the primary resource already
contains a suitable visual and whether it can be embedded, copied with
attribution, linked as an interactive companion, or shown through an approved
preview.

When the approved chapter contract includes a historical or current-development
lens, add only the history needed to explain the present mental model:

- compare the origin source with the current-state source;
- record the current version or the date the documentation was checked;
- separate protocol or format evolution from changing product usage patterns;
- treat provocative practitioner headlines as questions to investigate, not as
  conclusions or substitutes for primary evidence; and
- avoid a comprehensive timeline unless the user explicitly makes history part
  of the learning outcome.

## Follow the source well

When a source teaches the topic clearly:

- preserve its important causal sequence;
- preserve distinctions that make the explanation accurate;
- keep examples long enough to show the mechanism;
- simplify vocabulary only when meaning survives;
- identify product-specific behavior explicitly;
- avoid rearranging the material into a generic list of terms.

The chapter may use a different narrative, but the change must improve the
approved reader outcome rather than merely make the prose look original.

## Place references

Use direct, named links rather than bare numbers.

- Attach a claim-level citation immediately after the claim it supports.
- Link to the exact supporting section when practical.
- Open external citations in a new tab in the HTML book.
- Keep a complete reference list at the end of the chapter.
- Present one or two core companion readings near the opening when they can
  substitute for, or guide, the chapter.
- Keep Extended references separate and never duplicate cited sources there.
- Attribute external figures in their captions and keep a local source record
  when storing a copy.

Do not hide an unsupported broad claim behind several nearby links. Narrow the
claim or find the correct source.

## Review questions

Before approving a section, ask:

1. Which resource is teaching this section?
2. Are we following its mechanism accurately?
3. What did the book add, and why was it necessary?
4. Are product examples labeled as product examples?
5. Is each important claim referenced at the point of use?
6. Does this material belong in this chapter?
7. Did an attractive extra resource cause scope drift?
8. For a living standard or product, did we verify the current version and
   distinguish it from the origin story?
